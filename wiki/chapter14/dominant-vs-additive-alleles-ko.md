## Question (Translation)

I have a question. I am not sure whether dominant alleles and additive alleles can be clearly distinguished from one another. If a dominant allele is defined as "having one copy of a variant is enough to produce a noticeable effect," I am unsure whether a clear threshold for what constitutes a "noticeable effect" can actually be established. If we assign a value of 100 to the effect of achondroplasia and a value of 1 to the smallest-effect additive allele, there must be countless alleles with effects between 1 and 100—likely forming a continuum. From what level of effect can an allele be called dominant? I understand that additive alleles show a regular change of 0→1→2 as genotype goes from AA to AB to BB, while dominant alleles follow a 0→1→1 pattern—which seems to provide a distinction. But could it not be the case that the magnitude of change from AA to AB is not perfectly identical to that from AB to BB?

## Answer (Translation)

Good question. Let's start here: the additive and dominant cases described in textbooks and lectures are not rigid categories that cleanly divide reality, but rather two reference points created to aid understanding. Think of 0→1→2 as the extreme example of pure additivity, and 0→1→1 as the extreme example of pure dominance.

The core point is this: biology is not a discipline that cuts phenomena into sharp categories—it is a discipline that uses models to describe data. We describe things this way because certain patterns exist in the data, not because nature is organized into those boxes.

Whether something is additive or dominant is less a property of the allele itself and more a model for describing the pattern created by AA, AB, and BB. In practice, cases like 0→0.6→1.0 or 0→0.7→1.0 are far more common. Rather than forcing these into additive-or-dominant bins, we treat how far AB deviates from the midpoint as a continuous value—and interpret that as the dominance deviation.

In summary: additive and dominant are not two sharply separated categories but rather reference points at opposite ends of a spectrum. Most real-world variants fall somewhere in between, and we use these concepts to try to describe that continuum.
